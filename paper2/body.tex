\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A retail forecast is used for more than its number. When a merchant plans a
promotion, the question is not only how much will sell but how much of it the
promotion will add, and whether a deeper price cut would add more. Forecasting
models that see the plan can answer that directly: they forecast the item with
the plan as made, and again with nothing planned, and the difference is the lift
the plan adds. Our world model and lift model (paper~1) are built this way: each
forecast is a baseline under the nothing-planned plan times a lift, and the lift
is exactly zero when nothing is planned.

Such an explanation is useful only if it can be trusted, and being built into
the model does not make it right. The explanation literature separates two
properties~\cite{jacovi2020}: an explanation is \emph{faithful} if it reflects
what the model does, and \emph{correct} if it reflects what happens in the
world. In molecular property prediction, MolSanity found the two come apart:
attributions faithful to the model were often anti-aligned with the
substructure that determines the property~\cite{molsanity}. Retail has a
property chemistry lacks: some causes can be measured. Dominick's Finer Foods
moved store prices on purpose in the early 1990s~\cite{hoch1994}, so the
response of sales to a price change can be estimated from the data and the
model's stated response compared with it.

This paper audits the plan-lift explanations of every saved world-model and
lift-model version from paper~1 with eight checks (Figure~\ref{fig:audit}) and
asks whether versions whose explanations pass more checks also forecast
better.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Explanations and their evaluation}
Feature attributions such as LIME~\cite{ribeiro2016} and SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017},
with its exact form for trees~\cite{lundberg2020}, are the most used way to
explain a model's output~\cite{adadi2018}. Several studies show that they can
fail quietly: saliency maps that barely change when a network's weights are
randomised~\cite{adebayo2018}, attributions that move with input shifts the
model ignores~\cite{kindermans2019}, unstable explanations for near-identical
inputs~\cite{alvarezmelis2018}, and attribution rankings that do not survive
removing the features they rank highest~\cite{hooker2019}. Jacovi and
Goldberg~\cite{jacovi2020} set out faithfulness as a separate property from
plausibility; Rudin~\cite{rudin2019} argues for models that are interpretable
by construction instead of explained after the fact. Our models are of the
second kind for the plan, which lets the audit ask whether built-in
explanations behave better than post-hoc ones. For time series, explanation
methods and their benchmarks are reviewed by Rojat et al.~\cite{rojat2021};
Ismail et al.~\cite{ismail2020} and Tonekaboni et al.~\cite{tonekaboni2020}
show that saliency methods designed for images often fail on temporal data.

\paragraph{Price response as a measured cause}
Hoch, Dr\`eze and Purk~\cite{hoch1994} report Dominick's store-level pricing
experiments: in each of 19 categories, stores were assigned to higher, lower or
unchanged regular prices for 16 weeks, and category units moved by about 3\%
for a 10\% price change, an elasticity near $-0.4$. Montgomery~\cite{montgomery1997}
and Hoch et al.~\cite{hoch1995} model store-level price response on the same
data. The public files do not record which stores or weeks were in the
experiments; Abrams, Gui and Horta\c{c}su~\cite{abrams} reconstructed them with
the original documentation and found that some published price changes did not
replicate. We therefore use the price deviations visible in the public data as a
quasi-experiment and estimate their effect by difference in
differences~\cite{angrist2009}, with standard errors clustered by
store~\cite{bertrand2004,cameron2015}.

\section{What is explained, and the checks}
\label{sec:checks}

For each item, store and forecast period the audit records the forecast with the
plan as made; the forecast with nothing planned (each item at its regular price,
its highest over the last 12 periods, and every lever off); and each lever's
contribution, the forecast minus the forecast with that lever off and the rest of
the plan as made. Contributions need not add up to the total lift; the
shortfall is the interaction between levers, reported as such. For LightGBM the
same on/off contributions are computed, together with TreeSHAP values on its
lever inputs~\cite{lundberg2020}.

\begin{description}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item[C1 By construction.] Zero lift with nothing planned; a baseline that does not
move when the plan changes; baseline plus lift equal to the forecast. Largest
violation relative to the largest forecast.
\item[C2 Randomisation.] The lift readout, then the baseline readout, the
transition and the encoder are re-initialised in turn~\cite{adebayo2018}; rank
agreement between the original and randomised lifts should fall towards zero.
\item[C3 Seed stability.] Rank agreement of item-week lifts between seeds of the
same configuration, set against the agreement of their forecasts.
\item[C4 Price direction.] The share of item-weeks where a deeper price cut
gives a lower forecast than a shallower one.
\item[C5 Against what sold.] On promoted and price-cut item-weeks, the forecast
against sales, and the rank agreement of the predicted lift with sales over the
model's own baseline. Merchants promote in weeks they expect to sell well, so
this measures agreement, not cause.
\item[C6 Against LightGBM.] Rank agreement of each lever's contribution with
LightGBM's, on/off and TreeSHAP, on the same window.
\item[C7 Against a measured cause.] Described in \S\ref{sec:zones}.
\item[C8 Plan read-back.] From paper~1: the plan read back from the world
model's rolled state.
\end{description}

\section{Ground truth: zone price deviations on Dominick's}
\label{sec:zones}



Dominick's set regular prices by zone: within a zone every store was meant to
carry the same regular price~\cite{dominicks}. For each store and item we take
the log of the week's regular price (no deal code, not a temporary cut) over
that item's usual regular price in that store, and subtract the median of the
same across the zone's stores that week, so moves made zone-wide cancel. An
episode is at least 12 consecutive weeks in which this is at least 3\% in the
same direction for most of the store's items, with a median of at least 5\%;
controls are same-zone stores within 1\% of their zone over those weeks. The
detector was written, and its episodes listed, before any model was scored
against them. A first version that measured stores against the zone's most
common price found one episode in analgesics: in the experiments a third of a
zone's stores moved up and a third down, so the most common price was often a
moved one. The change is recorded, with its date, in the plan.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/fig_zones.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The price deviations cluster in 1992 and 1993, when the chain

but not scored. \textbf{(b)} For the \zScored{} scored episodes, the store's

experiments~\cite{hoch1994}.}
\label{fig:zones}
\end{figure*}

The detector finds \zEpisodes{} episodes in the six categories, \zScored{} with at
least three control stores (Figure~\ref{fig:zones}). Most lie between Dominick's
weeks 190 and 240, mid 1992 to mid 1993, the period of the chain's experiments;
the store prices sit \zSizeLo\% to \zSizeHi\% off their zone peers, in line with
the 6\% to 24\% changes the experiments made. Over the first 8 weeks of each
scored episode, category units move against price in \zSignShare\% of episodes,
and \zSigShare\% have an interval that excludes zero. The pooled slope of the
measured response on the price change is $\zSlope$, and the median episode
elasticity $\zMedianElasticity$, close to the $-0.4$ Hoch, Dr\`eze and Purk
report. Single episodes are noisy, and some move with price, as Abrams, Gui and
Horta\c{c}su found when they replicated the experiments.

For each scored episode the world model and LightGBM are fitted on the data up to
the week before it and forecast its first 8 weeks twice: with the store's prices
as they were, and with them moved back to its zone peers. The log ratio of the
two forecasts of category units is the model's stated response, compared with
the measured one.

\section{Model versions}
\label{sec:versions}

Every saved world-model and lift-model checkpoint from paper~1 is audited: the
development and final windows of Frat, the development versions on Dominick's,
and the M5 practice, confirmation and test windows. The Dominick's final world
models, an average of three fits, were not saved in paper~1; they are refitted
with settings unchanged for the audit, three seeds each, while paper~1 keeps the
scores of its original runs. Each version's accuracy is read from its own run
record. LightGBM is refitted on the Dominick's and Frat final windows and the M5
test month for C6.
